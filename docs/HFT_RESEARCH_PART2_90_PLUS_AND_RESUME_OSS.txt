================================================================================
 1. SUMMARY
================================================================================

* Features stop adding points at about 85. From there, each point has to be
  backed by evidence that someone else can check, or that someone else
  produced: packets on a real network path, timestamps taken outside your
  own code, a second implementation that agrees with yours, a stranger who
  reproduced your numbers, a maintainer who merged your code.

* The 90+ version of this project is a small trading system that replays a
  full real Nasdaq day over real multicast, quotes against its own
  exchange over binary order entry, measures tick-to-trade at the network
  card, survives injected faults under a deterministic simulator, and is
  released so that other people can reproduce every number. Section 3
  breaks that into fifteen upgrades. You need about nine of them.

* None of it needs paid data or special hardware to start. Nasdaq publishes
  sample full-day ITCH files, IEX publishes free daily packet captures,
  and Databento gives new accounts $125 of historical credit. Software
  packet timestamps from the Linux kernel work on ordinary network cards.

* For the resume, the strongest contributions are to projects that
  engineers at trading firms themselves maintain or use. Hudson River
  Trading's own open-source page names Compiler Explorer, Apache Arrow,
  pybind11, nanobind, Verilator and others. Point72, Man Group, Bloomberg
  and XTX Markets all publish C++ projects on GitHub.
  Section 6 ranks about 40 projects in four tiers.

* My recommended three: Apache Arrow C++ (structured onboarding, named by
  HRT), Point72's csp (a quant firm's own C++ engine, small team, your
  pull request gets seen), and QuantLib from Part 1. Add google/benchmark
  or HdrHistogram_c as a fourth because you will use them daily.

* Order still matters. Every upgrade here depends on Goals 1 to 4 from
  Part 1. Starting N-items before the README, the fixed-point migration,
  the ITCH decoder and the measurement harness exist would add scope to a
  project whose last code commit is 19 August.

 2. WHAT 90+ MEANS ON THIS SCALE
================================================================================

Scale (same as Part 1)
  100  production-grade open source written by specialists
   85  would stand out among professional engineers' personal work
   70  strong undergraduate portfolio project
   50  typical finished course or side project

Scorecard: today, after the six goals, and what 90+ requires
                                         weight  today  6 goals  90+
  Technical ambition and difficulty        15     7.0     8.0    9.0
  Breadth of stack                         15     8.0     8.5    9.0
  Core technical skill (low latency)       20     4.0     9.0    9.5
  Domain modelling and correctness         10     5.5     8.5    9.0
  C++ structure and craftsmanship          10     5.5     8.0    9.0
  Testing and verification                  5     5.0     8.5    9.5
  Engineering practice                      5     2.5     8.5    9.0
  Presentation and demo                    10     1.0     9.0    9.5
  Completion                               10     4.0     8.5    9.5
                                          ----   ----    ----   ----
  Weighted total                           100     50      85     92

What earns a 9 or better in each row
  Ambition      The problem includes something outside your own process:
                a real network path, real exchange data at full scale, a
                second venue, replication, or hardware.
  Breadth       Feed, book, strategy, risk, order entry, exchange,
                journal, recovery, measurement and demo all run together
                as one system, not as separately tested parts.
  Core skill    Latency measured at the network card, a lock-free
                structure with a written correctness argument, and at
                least three optimisations with before/after profiles,
                one of which failed and is reported anyway.
  Correctness   Your book agrees with an independent implementation over
                a full real day. Order-state rules are written as a
                model and checked.
  C++           A reviewer from a mature project has read and merged
                your C++. Interfaces are small, typed and documented.
                No undefined behaviour under any sanitizer.
  Testing       Deterministic simulation with fault injection running
                every night; fuzzers with a saved corpus; every bug ever
                found has a regression test with its seed.
  Practice      One command reproduces each published number. Benchmarks
                run on every change and fail on regression.
  Demo          A stranger understands it in 90 seconds and can run it
                in one step.
  Completion    Tagged release, documentation, licence, and at least
                one outside person who built it and reported back.

The pattern: the first 85 points are about what you built. The last ten are
about who else can confirm it.

 3. THE FIFTEEN UPGRADES
================================================================================

Each entry: what it is, why it moves the score, what counts as done, and
what it depends on. Nothing here is implemented.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PILLAR A -- A HARDER PROBLEM  (ambition, breadth)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

N1  Full-day multicast replay over a real network path
    What: an exchange-side replayer that reads a Nasdaq ITCH day, frames it
      in MoldUDP64 with sequence numbers and sends it over UDP multicast at
      wall-clock pace (and at 10x). Your feed handler receives it in a
      separate process, ideally on a separate machine or network port.
      Order entry goes back over OUCH on SoupBinTCP to your matching engine.
    Why: this turns a library into a system. It is the single change that
      most separates the top personal project (abt-t2t, Part 1 section 4)
      from the rest.
    Done when: a full session replays with zero gaps and zero drops, and
      the run prints packets sent, received, gaps detected, gaps recovered.
    Needs: Goals 2 and 3. Extends [D01 D02 D3 F01].

N2  Latency measured at the network card
    What: take receive and transmit timestamps from the kernel with
      SO_TIMESTAMPING instead of from your own code. Start with software
      timestamps, which work on any card. Move to hardware timestamps on a
      card that supports them (check with `ethtool -T <interface>`).
      Tick-to-trade becomes: transmit stamp of the order minus receive
      stamp of the packet that caused it.
    Then compare transports on identical traffic: blocking sockets, busy
      polling, io_uring, AF_XDP. AF_XDP has a generic copy mode that works
      on any device and a driver mode; zero-copy needs driver support, and
      the socket option XDP_OPTIONS_ZEROCOPY tells you which one you got.
      Report the mode you measured, not the mode you wanted.
    Why: it removes the largest weakness in any software benchmark, which
      is that the timer starts after the expensive part.
    Done when: docs/PERFORMANCE.md has a full-day histogram to p99.99 and
      max for at least two transports, with the timestamp source named.
    Needs: N1, Goal 4. Extends [C03 C6 D5 D6 I05].

N3  A second venue from real captured packets
    What: add IEX DEEP as a second market-data protocol. IEX publishes
      its DEEP and TOPS feeds as free daily pcap files, captured at the
      IEX transport layer, so you replay real exchange packets.
    Why: one protocol shows you can write a decoder. Two show the
      architecture is venue-neutral: same book, same strategy, same risk,
      different adapter. It also forces the adapter contract in [G04].
    Done when: the same engine binary runs a Nasdaq day and an IEX day
      selected by configuration, and both pass the conformance checks.
    Needs: Goal 3.

N4  Journal, snapshot and warm standby
    What: every input event is journaled before it is applied. A second
      engine process follows the journal. Kill the primary during replay;
      the standby takes over. Measure recovery time and prove no order was
      sent twice and none was lost.
    Why: the summary of Jane Street's public talk on building an exchange
      says that working in the single-digit microseconds helped them
      build a replicated system that is simple and robust.
      Deterministic single-threaded logic plus a log is the standard
      shape; showing failover on top of it is rare in personal projects.
    Done when: a scripted failover drill runs in CI and reports recovery
      time and a matching state hash.
    Needs: byte-identical replay [G2 J2]. Extends [H01 H02 H03 H09].

N5  Elective: FPGA ITCH parser
    What: an ITCH message parser in Verilog or SystemVerilog, simulated
      with Verilator and tested with cocotb, checked against your C++
      decoder on the same input. Synthesis for a board is optional.
    Why: Nasdaq's ITCH specification covers an FPGA version of the feed,
      and HRT's open-source page lists Verilator, cocotb and Slang among
      the projects its engineers work on. There is a Quartus-related
      folder in your home directory, so you may already have the tooling.
      Choose this only if hardware roles interest you; it is a
      specialisation, not a requirement for 90.
    Done when: simulated parser output matches the C++ decoder for a full
      file, with resource and timing estimates reported.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PILLAR B -- PROOF OF CORRECTNESS  (domain, testing)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

N6  Deterministic simulation testing, running every night
    What: feed handler, engine, gateway and exchange run in one process on
      a virtual clock. A seeded scheduler decides message order and
      injects loss, duplication, reordering, delay, disconnects and
      process restarts. Invariants are checked after every step.
    Model to copy: TigerBeetle's simulator. Their documentation states
      the two properties that matter: a failure reproduces exactly from a
      seed and a commit, and simulated time runs far faster than real
      time, so a minute of simulation covers days of behaviour.
    Done when: a nightly CI job runs N thousand seeds, any failing seed is
      filed automatically with its commit, and the README shows total
      seeds run and bugs found.
    Needs: single-threaded core and injected time [A08 J5]. Extends
      [F03 J4] and Part 1 section 6.6.

N7  Agreement with an independent implementation
    What: after every K messages of a full day, hash your book state and
      compare it with the state produced by a different codebase on the
      same file: CppTrader, itchbook, or a book built from Databento's
      normalised order-by-order data for the same date.
    Why: your own tests can only find the mistakes you thought of. A
      second implementation finds the ones you did not.
    Done when: zero unexplained differences over a full day, with any
      explained differences documented (for example hidden orders).
    Needs: Goal 3. Extends [C01 G6].

N8  A checked model of the order state machine
    What: write the OMS states and the gap-recovery protocol as a TLA+
      specification and model-check two properties: no fill is counted
      twice, and no accepted order is forgotten after a reconnect.
    Why: small (a few hundred lines) and almost never seen in a student
      project. It also gives you a precise answer to "how do you know the
      state machine is right?".
    Done when: the spec, the checker output and a note mapping each
      spec state to a C++ enum value are in the repository.
    Needs: [B02].

N9  Continuous fuzzing with a kept corpus
    What: libFuzzer, AFL++ or Google's FuzzTest on every decoder, the FIX
      and OUCH parsers and the journal reader. Corpus committed. Coverage
      reported. Every crash becomes a named regression input.
    Done when: fuzzers run in CI for a fixed budget per change and
      nightly for longer, under ASan and UBSan.
    Extends [C02 G1].

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PILLAR C -- PROOF OF PERFORMANCE  (core skill, engineering practice)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

N10 A performance lab with regression tracking
    What: one bare-metal Linux machine set up for measurement (isolated
      cores, interrupts moved, frequency fixed) and a benchmark job that
      runs on every merge, stores results over time and fails on
      regression. Tools such as Bencher or github-action-benchmark draw
      the history chart.
    Add: hardware counters beside every latency number, and three written
      case studies. One of the three must be an optimisation that did not
      help, with the evidence.
    Done when: the README links a chart of p50 and p99 over the last 50
      commits.
    Extends [C04 C06 E04 J9].

N11 Someone else reproduces your numbers
    What: one script builds, fetches the data sample, runs the benchmarks
      and writes a report. Ask one other person to run it on different
      hardware and publish their output next to yours. Joseph is the
      obvious first person to ask, and you can return the favour by
      filing the gradient bug from Part 1 on GustGrid with a fix.
    Why: "reproduced by a second party on different hardware" is a
      sentence almost no personal project can write.
    Done when: docs/PERFORMANCE.md has a table with two machines and two
      names.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PILLAR D -- OUTSIDE VERIFICATION  (craft, demo, completion)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

N12 Ship it as a product
    What: tagged v1.0, changelog, licence, documentation site, a dev
      container, and the two most reusable parts published as standalone
      libraries with vcpkg or Conan recipes: the SPSC queue and the ITCH
      decoder. Several standalone C++ ITCH parsers I checked were last
      touched in 2021-2022, and CppTrader's handler comes with its own
      dependency stack, so a tested, fuzzed, documented, dependency-free
      one could attract real users.
    Done when: someone you do not know opens an issue or a pull request.

N13 Publish the engineering
    What: three articles: the measurement method, one optimisation case
      study, and the simulation-testing story with a real bug it found.
      Post them where practitioners read. The author of abt-t2t (Part 1)
      posted that work on the quant and high-frequency-trading
      subreddits. Give a ten-minute talk at a local C++ meetup.
    Done when: three articles are public and linked from the README.

N14 Have professionals review your C++
    What: merged pull requests in two projects from section 6. This is
      the cheapest way to get your code read by engineers who do this for
      a living, and it is the only item here that someone else signs.
    Done when: two merged, non-trivial pull requests are linked from your
      resume.

N15 One research result on real data
    What: the latency-sensitivity study [I01] run on real order-by-order
      data rather than synthetic ticks: how fill quality and adverse
      selection change as cancel delay grows, with assumptions, fees and
      uncertainty stated. Negative or inconclusive results count.
    Done when: a short paper-style report with reproducible configs.
    Needs: backtester with queue position [I2 E9], N7.

  - Tests you cannot explain line by line.
  - Any of it built on the double-priced, mutex-guarded core as it stands
    today. The base has to be fixed first.


================================================================================
 5. DATA AND HARDWARE YOU CAN GET
================================================================================

Data
  Nasdaq sample ITCH days (free)
    Nasdaq hosts a public directory of sample TotalView-ITCH 5.0 files at
    emi.nasdaq.com/ITCH/Nasdaq ITCH/. One file listed there,
    01302019.NASDAQ_ITCH50.gz, is 4.76 GB compressed. Nasdaq's FAQ says
    5.0 files average 5-10 GB compressed and 15-25 GB uncompressed. Older
    tutorials give an ftp:// address for the same directory. The
    official historical product (rolling T+1 files) is a subscription
    service; you do not need it.
  IEX HIST (free)
    IEX offers DEEP and TOPS for download the day after trading, with the
    trailing twelve months kept available, as pcap files captured at the
    IEX transport protocol layer. Use is subject to the IEX Historical
    Data Terms of Use. Good for N3 and for packet-level replay.
  Databento (free credit, then paid)
    New accounts get $125 of historical-data credit, usable on Nasdaq
    TotalView-ITCH including order-by-order data. The credit is one per
    team and expires after six months, and a payment method is required
    at sign-up, so create the account only when N7 or N15 is ready to use
    it. Their C++ client is one of the contribution targets in Part 1.
  Rules for all three: read the terms before redistributing anything.
  Commit small fixtures you created yourself, never vendor files. Record
  the file name, date and checksum of every dataset behind a published
  number [D03].

Hardware
  Needed for N1, N2, N10: one Linux machine you control (not WSL, not a
    shared cloud VM) for measurement. A second machine or a second network
    port with a direct cable makes N1 and N2 much more convincing.
  Timestamps: run `ethtool -T` on each interface to see whether it
    reports hardware receive and transmit timestamping. Software
    timestamps are enough to begin.
  AF_XDP zero-copy depends on the card's driver. Check before buying.
  Solarflare cards with Onload and ef_vi are what abt-t2t used. They are
    the professional tool and are not required for 90. Treat them as a
    later purchase, after software measurement has shown the network
    stack is your bottleneck.
  FPGA (N5): simulation with Verilator needs no board.

Study material specific to this stage (beyond Part 1 section 7)
  Jane Street, "How to Build an Exchange" (talk and transcript).
  Jane Street, Signals and Threads podcast, "Multicast and the Markets"
    with Brian Nigito, 2020. Why exchanges are built on sequenced
    multicast.
  TigerBeetle documentation, "Deterministic Simulation Testing", and
    their 2026 post on protocol-aware simulation.
  Linux kernel documentation: networking/timestamping and
    networking/af_xdp.
  Nasdaq specifications: TotalView-ITCH 5.0, MoldUDP64, OUCH 5.0,
    SoupBinTCP.

 6. OPEN-SOURCE CONTRIBUTIONS RANKED FOR RESUME VALUE
================================================================================

How I ranked them
  Resume value = (who recognises the project) x (how hard the review is)
  x (chance of a merge within two months). A merged pull request in a
  project that trading-firm engineers maintain or depend on is worth more
  than ten in a project nobody on the interview panel has heard of.

Activity figures: shallow git clones on 16 Nov 2026; commits and distinct
authors since mid-May 2026, merge commits included. Approximate.

Evidence of what trading firms touch
  Hudson River Trading's open-source page names projects its engineers
  maintain or contribute to: Compiler Explorer (created by Matt Godbolt),
  Apache Arrow, pybind11, nanobind, corral, Slang, Verilator, cocotb,
  Polars, Ruff, mypy and others. A public leaderboard of quant-firm GitHub
  organisations (kadoa.com) has these C++ or C++-core projects near the
  top: Man Group's ArcticDB, XTX Markets' ternfs, Point72's csp and HRT's
  slang-server. Jane Street leads overall but publishes in OCaml.

TIER S -- PROJECTS PUBLISHED OR MAINTAINED BY TRADING FIRMS' ENGINEERS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

S1. csp                         github.com/Point72/csp        C++ / Python
    Point72's stream-processing library: a C++ graph engine driven from
    Python, used so research and production share one code path. Open
    since June 2024; Point72 said it published it partly to attract
    talent. Apache-2.0. ~70 commits, 6 authors.
    Why first: a hedge fund's own C++ engine, a small maintainer team, so
    a good pull request is read by the people you want to be read by.
    Start with: adapters (its wiki has listed Redis and websocket
    adapter work), documentation, build fixes on a platform they test
    less.

S2. Apache Arrow (C++)          github.com/apache/arrow       C++
    The columnar data format under most data tooling. Named on HRT's
    page. ~720 commits, 164 authors.
    Onboarding: an official New Contributor's Guide, and a
    "good-first-issue" label for issues meant to take a weekend or less.
    Start with: the guide's step-by-step first pull request, then compute
    kernels or the fuzzing targets.

S3. pybind11 and nanobind       github.com/pybind/pybind11    C++
                                github.com/wjakob/nanobind    C++
    The two libraries that bind C++ to Python. Both named on HRT's page.
    ~130 commits / 28 authors and ~280 / 22.
    Why: every quant stack has a C++ core and Python research on top.
    Binding your own order book to Python with nanobind is a useful
    project feature and the natural way to find something to fix.
    Level: heavy template code. Good for C++ depth, hard for a first PR.

S4. Compiler Explorer           github.com/compiler-explorer/compiler-explorer
    The tool every C++ engineer uses, created by an HRT engineer.
    ~620 commits, 152 authors: the most open contributor base on this
    list. Mostly TypeScript, so it shows engagement with the C++
    community rather than C++ skill. Adding a library or a compiler
    configuration is a common first contribution.

S5. ArcticDB                    github.com/man-group/ArcticDB C++ core
    Man Group's time-series database; the most-starred C++ project from a
    quant firm. ~220 commits, 16 authors. Check the licence first.

S6. BlazingMQ                   github.com/bloomberg/blazingmq  C++
    From Part 1. Still the best-documented newcomer path of any
    finance-firm C++ project ("good first issue" label, DCO sign-off).

S7. Verilator, Slang, slang-server                             C++
    github.com/verilator/verilator       ~1,500 commits, 135 authors
    github.com/MikePopoloski/slang       ~370 commits, 37 authors
    github.com/hudson-trading/slang-server  ~160 commits, 19 authors
    Hardware-design tools written in C++. Slang's founder and the
    slang-server project are at HRT. Only relevant if you take the FPGA
    elective (N5), in which case they are the best targets on the list.

S8. Harder, for later
    github.com/XTXMarkets/ternfs    C++ distributed file system, 8 authors
    github.com/hudson-trading/corral  C++ coroutine library, ~5 commits
    github.com/aeron-io/aeron        from Part 1

TIER A -- WIDELY RECOGNISED HIGH-PERFORMANCE C++ WITH REAL ONBOARDING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A1. ClickHouse                  github.com/ClickHouse/ClickHouse   C++
    Column-store database. Its developer documentation links directly to
    open issues labelled "easy task", every contributor is recorded in a
    system table, a bot handles the CLA, and each pull request runs
    about 50 CI jobs. Tests start once an employee marks the pull request
    as testable.
    Cost: the repository and build are very large. Budget a weekend just
    to build it.

A2. DuckDB                      github.com/duckdb/duckdb           C++
    In-process analytical database. Extremely active, 300+ authors in six
    months. Clean modern C++ and fast review. Good for vectorised
    execution and data-structure work.

A3. LLVM                        github.com/llvm/llvm-project       C++
    Many open "good first issue" items at any time, a lot of them small
    missed optimisations in the optimiser. The deepest C++ and
    compiler education available, and universally recognised.

A4. fmt                         github.com/fmtlib/fmt              C++
    The formatting library behind std::format. ~260 commits, 67 authors.
    Small, exemplary modern C++, very responsive maintainer.

A5. RocksDB                     github.com/facebook/rocksdb        C++
    ~490 commits, 46 authors. Storage engine used inside many databases.
    Relevant to your journal and tick store.

A6. Seastar family              C++20
    github.com/scylladb/seastar       from Part 1
    github.com/redpanda-data/redpanda ~1,700 commits, 55 authors
    github.com/scylladb/scylladb      thousands of commits, 74 authors
    Thread-per-core systems. Closest in design to a trading engine among
    the large projects. Steep.

A7. Smaller and friendlier
    github.com/apache/kvrocks        C++, ~140 commits, 38 authors
    github.com/dragonflydb/dragonfly C++, ~950 commits, 47 authors
    github.com/catchorg/Catch2       C++, ~80 commits, 18 authors
    github.com/google/fuzztest       C++, ~190 commits, 14 authors
    Kvrocks is an Apache project with a high ratio of authors to commits,

A8. Networking
    github.com/xdp-project/xdp-tools  C, ~30 commits, 10 authors
    github.com/DPDK/dpdk              C, ~1,900 commits, 201 authors

    github.com/axboe/liburing         from Part 1
    Natural targets while doing N2.

WHAT MAKES A CONTRIBUTION COUNT ON A RESUME
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Counts
    - A bug fix with a test that fails before and passes after.
    - A measured performance improvement, with the benchmark included.
    - A feature the maintainers asked for in an issue.
    - Sustained presence: three pull requests in one project beat one
      each in three.
  Does not count, and can hurt
    - Typo and whitespace pull requests listed as "contributions".
    - Large unsolicited rewrites.
    - Pull requests you cannot defend line by line. Maintainers reject
      these quickly, and GCC states outright that proposals written
      entirely with generative AI are rejected. Use AI to understand a
      codebase; write and own the change yourself.
  How to write it
    "Apache Arrow (C++): fixed <what> in <component>, added regression
     test (merged, PR #N)" with a link. One line per project, strongest
     first, under an "Open Source" heading.

Google Summer of Code 2027
  A paid summer project inside an open-source organisation with a mentor.
  People commonly list it on a resume alongside internships.
  Dates: Google had not announced the 2027 timeline as of mid-November
    2026. The 2026 cycle ran: organisations announced 19 February,
    applications 16-31 March, coding from 25 May, 185 organisations.
    Expect the same shape.
  Eligibility in 2026: 18 or older, and a student or a newcomer to open
    source.
  C and C++ organisations in 2026 included GCC and CERN-HSF, an umbrella
    for physics software that has hosted C++ tooling projects such as
    Clad and CppInterOp. The 2027 list appears in February; check it for
    LLVM, Boost and the projects in section 6.
  How selection works in practice: organisations favour applicants who
    are already contributing. GCC's own guidance says contributions and
    community interaction weigh heavily when candidates are chosen. That
    is the reason to start in November rather than March.
  Plan: pick the organisation by January, have two merged pull requests
    there by the time organisations are announced, discuss your proposal
    with a mentor before the application window opens.

Others to check for eligibility and dates
  MLH Fellowship. G-Research, a quant firm, funds an open-source
    fellowship track through Major League Hacking.
  LFX Mentorship (Linux Foundation) and Outreachy run on similar lines.
  I did not verify 2027 terms for these three.
